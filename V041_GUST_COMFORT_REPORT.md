# v0.4.1 Urban Gust and Passenger-Comfort Update

## Why this update was needed

The v0.4.0 dashboard exposed two confusing behaviours:

1. A selected discrete gust could look like steady wind if the scenario was enabled after its absolute simulation-time window had already passed.
2. The first takeoff jerk could be dominated by a diagnostic artifact: the force-model acceleration contained gravity while the ground constraint held the actual velocity at zero; differentiating that discontinuity at lift-off produced a large non-kinematic jerk spike.

v0.4.1 addresses both issues and separates two different passenger-comfort questions: transient UAM maneuver motion and sustained whole-body vibration.

## Wind scenarios

The dashboard now distinguishes:

- **Steady wind**: constant 3-D wind vector.
- **Single discrete gust**: one finite smooth gust event for standardized disturbance-response testing.
- **Repeated gusts**: recurring gust events with deterministic variation in timing, amplitude and direction.
- **Continuous turbulence**: continuously varying, correlated 3-D stochastic disturbance generated by a seeded filtered process.
- **Wind shear**: altitude-dependent disturbance.
- **Urban wake proxy**: finite multi-frequency 3-D wake encounter.

The delay control is now referenced to **scenario enable time**, not simulation t=0. If the aircraft is at t=70 s when the scenario is enabled and the delay is 2 s, the disturbance starts around t=72 s.

Repeated gusts and continuous turbulence are reduced-order controller-test models. They are not certified Dryden/Von-Karman turbulence implementations or CFD-resolved building wakes.

## Takeoff acceleration and jerk

The dashboard now uses kinematic acceleration from the actual integrated velocity:

`a[k] = (v[k] - v[k-1]) / dt`

and jerk from the kinematic acceleration:

`j[k] = (a[k] - a[k-1]) / dt`.

This respects the ground constraint and removes the artificial ground-to-liftoff spike caused by differentiating an unconstrained force-model acceleration.

The comfort guard is also upgraded. In v0.4.0 it only changed `max_accel`, and the vertical controller did not consistently apply that global value. In v0.4.1 the guard:

- applies the acceleration limit to all flight phases, including vertical takeoff and landing;
- applies a slew-rate limit to the commanded acceleration, so the command-level jerk is limited as well.

Actual vehicle jerk can still exceed the command limit under gusts, actuator dynamics, phase switching or model nonlinearities. The measured jerk therefore remains an output KPI.

## Recommended comfort indicators

There is no FAA/EASA eVTOL passenger-comfort regulation that establishes one universal acceleration or jerk certification limit. NASA's 2025 UAM passenger study explicitly notes that established UAM passenger-comfort guidelines do not yet exist.

For this project the recommended dashboard metrics are therefore deliberately split into two evidence-based categories.

### A. Sustained vibration: ISO 2631-1-style weighted RMS

Use the combined frequency-weighted RMS acceleration as the primary sustained-vibration KPI. For a seated passenger, the supporting seat-surface translational directions are treated as:

- x: `Wd`, multiplier 1.0
- y: `Wd`, multiplier 1.0
- z: `Wk`, multiplier 1.0

The project uses **0.315 m/s^2** as a design target because the ISO 2631-1 comfort interpretation commonly describes values below 0.315 m/s^2 as "not uncomfortable". This is a standard-based comfort-band boundary, not an aviation certification limit.

### B. Short eVTOL/UAM transients: NASA-informed engineering targets

NASA's 2025 sudden-heave UAM passenger study selected peak heave acceleration as the primary study factor and peak heave jerk as a secondary factor. The four replay conditions were:

| Run | RMS heave accel (m/s^2) | Peak heave accel (m/s^2) | Peak heave jerk (m/s^3) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 0.37 | 0.86 | 2.24 |
| B (minimum motion) | 0.17 | 0.45 | 1.09 |
| C (maximum motion) | 0.51 | 1.37 | 4.32 |
| D | 0.31 | 0.75 | 2.44 |

The v0.4.1 defaults are therefore conservative engineering targets:

- transient acceleration target: **0.50 m/s^2**
- transient jerk target: **1.50 m/s^3**

They intentionally lie near/above NASA's minimum-motion condition and below the medium-motion cases. They are **not NASA regulatory limits** and they are not presented as thresholds proven to guarantee comfort. The current implementation applies these values conservatively to the translational vector magnitude, which also ensures the heave component cannot exceed the same target without triggering the metric.

The 10 deg/s angular-rate value remains a user-selected study threshold. It is retained as a diagnostic KPI but is not labelled as a sourced regulation.

## ISO 2631-style frequency weighting

Whole-body vibration discomfort depends on frequency and direction, not only raw acceleration magnitude. ISO 2631 therefore applies frequency-dependent weighting before RMS calculation.

The implementation follows the published ISO 8041 Wd/Wk filter structure:

1. band-limiting high-pass section (f1 = 0.4 Hz);
2. band-limiting low-pass section (f2 = 100 Hz);
3. acceleration-velocity transition section;
4. for Wk, an additional upward-step shaping section.

Published filter parameters include:

- `Wd`: f3=f4=2 Hz, Q4=0.63;
- `Wk`: f3=f4=12.5 Hz, Q4=0.63, f5=2.37 Hz, Q5=0.91, f6=3.35 Hz, Q6=0.91.

The continuous-time transfer-function sections are cascaded to form H(s), then converted to a digital filter H(z) with a bilinear transform. The dashboard processes x/y with Wd and z with Wk, computes rolling RMS for the weighted axes, and combines them as a vector sum.

Because the flight simulation currently runs at 100 Hz (Nyquist 50 Hz), it cannot reproduce the full measurement bandwidth of a standards-compliant ISO 8041 vibration instrument. Consequently the dashboard calls this an **ISO 2631-style low-frequency engineering approximation**. A formal compliance implementation would use a sufficiently higher sample rate and validate the digital response against ISO 8041-1 tolerance requirements.
